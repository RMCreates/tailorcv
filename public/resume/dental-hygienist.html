How to tailor a dental hygienist resume to the job posting

Dental practices hire hygienists on license and permits first, then on how smoothly you run a hygiene schedule and how well patients respond to you. Your resume should answer the licensing questions immediately and then show the clinical breadth of your work.

What recruiters and ATS filters look for

List your RDH license and state, your degree (associate or bachelor's in dental hygiene), and the permits that matter in your state: local anesthesia, nitrous oxide administration, laser certification, and any expanded functions. Add CPR/BLS with the renewal date. If you hold licenses in more than one state, say so; practices near state lines look for it.

For each practice, describe the setting (general, periodontal, pediatric, public health, corporate DSO), the number of patients per day, the length of hygiene appointments and the equipment: ultrasonic scalers, digital radiography, intraoral cameras and scanners, and the practice management software (Dentrix, Eaglesoft, Open Dental, Curve). Note the procedures you perform regularly: prophylaxis, scaling and root planing, periodontal charting and maintenance, sealants, fluoride, whitening, impressions.

Dentists and office managers also look for patient education, re-care scheduling and case acceptance for periodontal treatment, because those drive the hygiene department's health. Mention infection control (OSHA, CDC guidelines) and any mentoring of assistants or new hygienists.

Keywords and skills to include (when they're true for you)

Only use the ones you can back up with real experience, and prefer the exact wording of the posting you're applying to.

  • RDH license
  • Local anesthesia permit
  • Nitrous oxide administration
  • Scaling and root planing
  • Periodontal charting and maintenance
  • Digital radiographs
  • Dentrix / Eaglesoft / Open Dental
  • Patient education
  • Infection control (OSHA / CDC)
  • Sealants and fluoride

Common mistakes on dental hygienist resumes

  • Burying your license, permits and renewal dates when they are the first thing the office checks.
  • Not naming the practice software, which many offices consider a time-to-productivity issue.
  • Listing only "cleanings" when you perform periodontal therapy, anesthesia and radiographs.
  • Ignoring the patient-education and scheduling side of the role.
