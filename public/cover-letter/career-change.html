How to write a career change cover letter

A career change cover letter has one job: make the hiring manager believe that your past work transfers to this role before they look at a resume that says otherwise. It should be short, specific and about them, not a story about your journey.

What the reader is looking for

Recruiters reading a career changer's application are asking two questions: why this field, and what can you do on day one? Answer both in the first half of the letter. Name the role, say plainly that you are moving from one field to another, and give the one or two concrete reasons that make the move credible: a certification you completed, a project you ran in the new domain, volunteer or freelance work, or parts of your old job that were already this job.

Then translate. Pick the two or three requirements in the posting that matter most and show, with a specific example each, that you have done something equivalent. A teacher moving into corporate training has planned curricula and managed groups; a restaurant manager moving into operations has run scheduling, inventory and P&L. Use the posting's own words where they are honestly accurate.

A structure that works

  1. Opening (2–3 sentences): the role, where you are coming from, and the single strongest reason you are a credible fit now.
  2. Evidence (1–2 short paragraphs): two or three posting requirements, each matched to a concrete thing you did, with a number where you have one.
  3. Why this move and this company (2–3 sentences): what you have already done to prepare, and why this employer specifically.
  4. Close (1–2 sentences): a direct request for a conversation and thanks. No apologies for the career change.

Example cover letter

This example is fictional: the person, employer and figures are invented to show the structure. Do not copy it; replace every detail with your own.

Dear Hiring Manager,

I'm applying for the Learning & Development Coordinator role at Harlow Peak Logistics. I have spent six years as a high-school science teacher and am moving into corporate training, and I think the overlap is more direct than it looks.

Your posting asks for someone who can design onboarding content, run sessions for groups of 10–30, and measure whether the training worked. I have built two full-year curricula from scratch, taught five sections a day, and used pre- and post-assessments to adjust lessons; last year my students' average on the state exam rose 11 points. I also led our department's new-teacher mentoring program, which is the part of my job I would most like to do full time.

To prepare for this move I completed the ATD Instructional Design certificate and built an onboarding module for a local nonprofit's volunteers. I would welcome the chance to show you that work and talk about how I could support your warehouse and driver onboarding program.

Thank you for your consideration.

Dana Okafor

Common mistakes

  • Opening with a long explanation of why you are leaving your old field. One sentence is enough.
  • Listing transferable "soft skills" instead of specific, comparable things you have done.
  • Apologizing for a lack of direct experience, which invites the reader to agree.
  • Sending the same letter to every posting instead of matching two or three of its requirements.
