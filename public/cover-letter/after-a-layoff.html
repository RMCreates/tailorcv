How to write a cover letter after a layoff

A layoff is common and most hiring managers do not hold it against you. The cover letter should mention it in one neutral sentence, if at all, and spend the rest of its space on what you did in the job you lost and what you would do in the one you want.

What the reader is looking for

Decide first whether to mention the layoff. If your most recent role ended recently and the gap is obvious, a single factual sentence ("My position was eliminated in a company-wide reduction in March") removes the question from the reader's mind. If the layoff was a while ago and you have done other things since, you may not need to mention it in the letter at all; it can come up in the interview.

Everything else should read like any strong cover letter: name the role, show two or three specific results from your recent work that match the posting, and explain why this company. Avoid any bitterness about the former employer and avoid over-explaining. Confidence here is simply describing your work clearly.

A structure that works

  1. Opening (2–3 sentences): the role, your background in one line, and the optional single sentence about the layoff.
  2. Results (1 paragraph): two or three outcomes from your most recent role that map to the posting's requirements.
  3. Why this role and company (2–3 sentences): a specific reason, plus anything you have done since the layoff that is relevant (a course, contract work, a certification).
  4. Close (1–2 sentences): request a conversation and thank them.

Example cover letter

This example is fictional: the person, employer and figures are invented to show the structure. Do not copy it; replace every detail with your own.

Dear Hiring Team,

I'm applying for the Supply Chain Analyst position at Coppervale Foods. I spent four years as a demand planning analyst at a regional beverage distributor until my role was eliminated in a restructuring in May.

In that role I owned the weekly forecast for about 1,200 SKUs across three warehouses, improved forecast accuracy from 71% to 83% over two years by rebuilding our seasonal models, and worked with purchasing to cut excess inventory on slow-moving items by roughly $600K. Your posting asks for SAP IBP and advanced Excel experience; I used both daily and built the reporting pack our operations director still uses.

Since May I have completed the APICS CPIM Part 1 exam and done a short contract building a safety-stock model for a local co-packer, which is the kind of problem your posting describes. Coppervale's move into direct-store delivery is exactly the type of planning challenge I want to work on.

I would appreciate the chance to talk through how I could contribute. Thank you for your consideration.

Jordan Whitfield

Common mistakes

  • Explaining the layoff at length, or sounding resentful about the former employer.
  • Leaving an unexplained recent end date that the reader will wonder about.
  • Sounding apologetic or desperate; describe your results the way you would have before the layoff.
  • Not mentioning what you have done since, when a course or contract would strengthen the case.
