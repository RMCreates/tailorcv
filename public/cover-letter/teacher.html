How to write a teacher cover letter

Principals read teacher cover letters quickly and look for certification, grade and subject match, and evidence that you can manage a classroom and move student outcomes. Keep the letter concrete and tied to the school, not a philosophy of education.

What the reader is looking for

State your certification and endorsements, the grades and subjects you have taught, and the kind of schools you have worked in, within the first few sentences. Hiring committees need to confirm you are eligible for the posting before anything else. If you hold additional endorsements (ESL, special education, reading) or dual certification, say so; those often decide between otherwise similar candidates.

Then give evidence. Describe your classroom in specifics: class sizes, student population, curricula and assessment tools, and results such as growth on district benchmarks, pass rates, attendance or behavioral improvements. Mention what you do beyond the classroom, such as coaching, clubs, curriculum committees or family engagement, since schools hire for the whole community. Finally, show you know this school: its programs, population or initiatives. Generic letters sent to every district are easy to spot and usually ignored.

A structure that works

  1. Opening (2–3 sentences): the position, your certification and endorsements, and your grade and subject experience.
  2. Classroom evidence (1 paragraph): your current or most recent assignment, methods, and two or three concrete results.
  3. Contribution beyond the classroom and fit with this school (1 short paragraph).
  4. Close (1–2 sentences): a request for an interview and thanks.

Example cover letter

This example is fictional: the person, employer and figures are invented to show the structure. Do not copy it; replace every detail with your own.

Dear Principal Okonkwo,

I'm applying for the Grade 7 Mathematics position at Ridgeback Middle School. I hold a state professional teaching license in secondary mathematics (grades 6–12) with an ESL endorsement, and I have taught middle-school math for five years in a Title I district.

This year I teach five sections of Grade 7 math and one section of pre-algebra, with class sizes of 26–30 and about a third of students classified as English learners. I use the district's Illustrative Mathematics curriculum with structured small-group rotations and weekly formative checks. Over the past two years, my students' average growth on the MAP assessment has exceeded the district target each year, and the share of my English learners meeting grade-level benchmarks rose from 38% to 61%.

Outside class I coach the MATHCOUNTS team and serve on the school's grading-reform committee. I'm drawn to Ridgeback because of its dual-language program and its emphasis on standards-based grading, which matches how I already run my classroom.

I would welcome the chance to interview and to share a sample lesson. Thank you for your time.

Rebecca Thornbury

Common mistakes

  • Opening with your teaching philosophy instead of certification, grade and subject.
  • No student outcome data, class sizes or curriculum names.
  • A generic letter with nothing specific to the school or district.
  • Running past one page; principals read dozens of these.
