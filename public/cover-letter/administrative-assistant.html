How to write an administrative assistant cover letter

Administrative assistant postings get a lot of similar applications, and the letter is where you can stand out by being specific about the scope you have handled and the judgment you have shown. It is also a direct test of the writing and attention to detail the job requires.

What the reader is looking for

State the kind of office and the people you supported: a department of 30, three directors, a single executive, a medical or legal office. Then describe scope with specifics: calendar and travel management, expense reporting, meeting and event coordination, document preparation, vendor and supply ordering, onboarding support, phones and front desk, and the software you use (Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Concur, Expensify, DocuSign, Zoom, Slack, a CRM or practice management system). Typing speed and bilingual ability are worth a line if the posting mentions them.

Hiring managers also look for discretion, prioritization and problem-solving. Give one brief example: rebooking an executive's travel during a disruption, coordinating a board meeting, fixing a process that kept failing. Tie your letter to the posting and the company; a letter that mentions their industry or a detail from the job description shows the attention to detail you are claiming. Proofread it twice.

A structure that works

  1. Opening (2 sentences): the role and your administrative background with the type of office and people supported.
  2. Scope and tools (1 paragraph): the functions you handle and the software you use, with numbers where possible.
  3. One example (3–4 sentences): a situation that shows judgment, prioritization or process improvement.
  4. Close (2 sentences): why this company, and a request to talk.

Example cover letter

This example is fictional: the person, employer and figures are invented to show the structure. Do not copy it; replace every detail with your own.

Dear Ms. Petrov,

I'm applying for the Administrative Assistant position supporting the Finance department at Wrenfield Architects. I have five years of administrative experience, most recently supporting a CFO and two directors at a 120-person engineering firm.

I manage three calendars, book domestic and international travel, process about 40 expense reports a month in Concur, prepare board and monthly reporting packets, and coordinate quarterly all-hands meetings for 120 people. I work daily in Microsoft 365, Teams, DocuSign and Concur, and I type about 75 words per minute.

Last year our board meeting fell on the day of a regional airport closure. I rebooked two directors onto trains, moved the meeting start by 90 minutes, reissued the packet with the updated agenda and confirmed the video link with remote members, so the meeting ran without incident. I also rebuilt our expense submission checklist, which cut rejected reports by about half.

I'm interested in Wrenfield because of its project-based work, which I understand means busy, deadline-driven periods, and I would welcome the chance to discuss the role. Thank you.

Hannah Oyelaran

Common mistakes

  • Generic claims of being organized and detail-oriented with no scope, numbers or tools.
  • Any typo or formatting slip, which disqualifies candidates for this role in particular.
  • Not naming who you supported or the size of the office.
  • A letter with nothing specific to the company or the department it names.
