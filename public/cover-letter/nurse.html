How to write a nursing cover letter

Nurse recruiters read cover letters for three things: license and certifications, clinical fit with the unit, and signs that you will stay. A short letter that states those plainly beats a long one about your calling to nursing.

What the reader is looking for

Open with your credentials and the unit you are applying to. State your license (RN or LPN, state or compact), years of experience, your current unit type and patient population, and the certifications the posting asks for (BLS, ACLS, PALS, specialty certifications). Nurse managers filling a specific unit want to know within two sentences whether your background matches.

Then give evidence of clinical competence and the things units care about: patient ratios you handle, EHR experience, precepting or charge duties, quality or safety work, and how you work with the interdisciplinary team. If you are changing specialties or are a new graduate, name your clinical rotations, capstone or residency and show why this unit. End with a specific reason for this hospital or facility, such as a Magnet designation, a residency program, its patient population or your ties to the community, which signals retention.

A structure that works

  1. Opening (2–3 sentences): the position and unit, your license, years of experience and current unit type.
  2. Clinical fit (1 paragraph): certifications, patient population, ratios, EHR and two specific contributions such as precepting, committee work or a quality project.
  3. Why this facility (2–3 sentences): a concrete reason tied to the facility or unit.
  4. Close (1–2 sentences): availability, a request to discuss the role and thanks.

Example cover letter

This example is fictional: the person, employer and figures are invented to show the structure. Do not copy it; replace every detail with your own.

Dear Ms. Delgado,

I'm applying for the Registered Nurse position on the Progressive Care Unit at Silvercreek Regional Medical Center. I am an RN with a compact license and four years of experience on a 28-bed telemetry unit, with current BLS, ACLS and PCCN certification.

On my current unit I care for four to five patients per shift, including post-cardiac-catheterization and heart-failure patients on continuous monitoring and titrated drips. I have precepted three new graduates through orientation, serve on the unit's practice council and helped introduce a standardized handoff that reduced reported communication errors on our floor. I chart in Epic and have used it for medication administration and telemetry documentation for the full four years.

Silvercreek's nurse residency and its reputation for keeping experienced nurses at the bedside are why I'm applying here rather than closer to home. I'm available for nights or rotating shifts and can start after a standard notice period.

Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome the chance to meet the PCU team.

Ana Lucía Ferrer, RN

Common mistakes

  • Opening with why you became a nurse instead of your license, certifications and unit experience.
  • Not naming the unit or patient population you are applying to.
  • Leaving out EHR, ratios and certifications the posting lists.
  • Giving no reason for choosing this facility, which makes managers worry about turnover.
